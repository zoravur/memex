## SECTION 1: Focused vs Diffuse Thinking

### Lesson 1: Introduction to the Focused and Diffuse Modes

- If we understand some basic neuroscience, we can learn better.
- 2 modes of thinking - focused and diffuse
  - focused -- when we concentrate intently on something.
  - diffuse -- related to neural resting states.
- Analogy: game of pinball
  - A thought is like a ball.
  - Thought patterns are like the bumpers in the pinball machine.
  - If the bumpers/pins are very close together, it's like we are in the focused mode.
    - Because the bumpers are close together, thoughts cannot travel very far without hitting one and being redirected.
    - Thus, thoughts stay very localized in one area of the machine -- our focus does not waver, and there are no leaps in logic or really novel ideas.
    - The bumpers block access to different parts of the machine, which represent distal thought patterns.
  - Conversely, if the bumpers are very far apart, it's like we are in the diffuse mode.
    - In the diffuse mode, it's difficult to focus in and be detail oriented, but our thoughts (the ball) is able to travel to different areas of the pinball machine easily. This is good for when we're trying to solve difficult problems. Oftentimes we need a thought pattern that we are unfamiliar with, and the way to access it is through the diffuse mode.
- As far as we know, we cannot be in both the focused and diffuse mode at once.

### Lesson 3: Using the Focused and Diffuse Modes

- Dali was a surrelaist painter.
- His technique was to relax and let his mind go free, often still vaguely thinking about what he was previously focused on. He would have a key in his hand, dangling above th efloor, and as he would fall asleep he would release the key and the clatter would wake him up, just in time to gather those diffuse mode thoughts, bringing them into his next focused bout of work.
- Thomas Edison did the same thing, except it was ball bearings.
- When you're learning something more difficult, your mind needs to be able to go back and forth between the two modes. Analogous to lifting weights, you have to give learning time to build on itself.
- Allows you to build a scaffold to hang your thinking on, a little bit every day.

### Lesson 4: What is learning?
- Brain weights 3 pounds, but consumes 10 times more calories than the rest of the body by weight, making it an expensive organ.
- The brain is complex, doing things that computers still cannot do (mostly operate in the physical world), without any sort of conscious thought most of the time.
- Because we are not aware of our brains and how they work, we rely on neuroimaging to understand what's really going on in there.
- Different areas of the brain are on depending on whether we are active or inactive.
- While inactive:
  - the default mode network is working.
  - Other areas of the brain are also working, much like the default mode network, but because they light up at not precisely the same time, we know that they serve slightly different purposes we don't fully understand.
- The old view of the brain is that synapess do not change much after maturity, excluding brain damage. But now we know that brain connectivity changes even into maturity.
  - Synapses change ("turn over") constantly, even nightly
- Synapses are 20 microns in diameter.
- You are not the same person after a night's sleep, you get an upgrade.

## SECTION 2: Procrastination, Memory, and Sleep

### Lesson 1: A Procrastination Preview

- Everybody has some issues with procrastination
- We will talk about
  - Why procrastination arises
  - How to address procrastination
- When you look at something you don't want to do, you activate the area of your brain associated with pain (the insular cortex)
- Your brain looks for a way to stop that negative stimulation, by switching to something else.
- However, research shows that not long after people start doing the thing that they were procrastinating on, the feeling disappears.
- When you procrastinate, you
  - observe
  - get a cue about something that causes unease
  - To fix the unease, you turn your attention away from whatever caused it.
  - As a result, you feel happier. Temporarily.
- Pomodoro technique
  - Can help overcome procrastination
  - Set a timer to 25 minutes. Stop all interruptions and focus.
    - Almost anybody can focus for 25 minutes.
  - After 25 minutes, take 2-5 minutes to retrieve what you've just learned. Recall, by pulling the ideas from your own mind by writing them down or saying them out loud. Only peak at your notes if you have to.
    - This type of retrieval practice strengthens the memory traces, making them much easier to find later.
  - After recalling, do 2-3 minutes of "restful wakefulness"
  - During this time, DO NOT pick up your phone, as this jolts you into the focused mode.
  - Instead, rest quietly, do something undemanding, like making tea or cleaning, or going outside / on a walk.
  - This gives your brain a chance to move more into the diffuse mode.
  - In summary, 25 minutes focused, 2-5 minutes diffuse recall, 2-3 minutes of restful wakefulness.
- During the rest period, your brain replays pattern sfrom what you've just studied.

### Lesson 2: Practice makes Permanent.
- Math and science are abstract, which makes them hard to learn and remember.
- It is important to practice, therefore, to enhance and strengthen the neural connections.
- The more bastract, the more practice is necessary.
- When you first learn something, the neural pattern is there but very faint.
- When you solve the problem again, without aids, the neural pattern becomes a little bit stronger, a little darker.
- It gets even darker and more ingrained when you have it down cold, with no warmup. That's the point you want to get to.
- **Practice makes permanent.**
- Start by focusing intently. Then take a break, or change your focus for a little while.
- During this time of relaxation, the diffuse mode works in the background, and helps you out with your conceptual understanding.
- Analogy -- building a wall
  - Learning something new is like building a wall. Each layer of mortar needs a chance to dry before placing the next layer of bricks, otherwise it's extremely difficult to prevent the facts from getting all jumbled.
- Do a little bit every day. If you procrastinate, use Pomodoro.
- Brief period seach day will help you start building the foundation that will help you build the neural patterns for more challenging materials.

### Lesson 3: Introduction to Memory
- Long term memory: Associated with vocabulary, memorized equations, etc. It is knowledge you have. On the other hand,
- Working memory: Memory that is used in order to hold ideas in your mind or attention.
- It's what you can manipulate easily; your working set of data; your RAM, or your blackboard.
- Lots of ways to categorize memory, but for this course we talk only about long term and working memory.
- Working memory is asociated with the prefrontal cortex.
- It is believed that working memory can only hold about 4 chunks of information.
  - What we thinking is more, is actually us chunking many small items into 4 or fewer larger items.
- Repetition is needed so that "natural disippating processes" don't "suck" the memory away.
- You might close your eyes to prevent distraction.
- Short term memory is like a bad blackboard.
- Long term memory is like a warehouse, distribueted over a large area. Different types of memories are stored in different areas of the brain.
- There can be so many items in long term memory that they can bury each other.
- Repetition assists wtith retrival. Repeat at least a few times.
- Long term memory is where concepts are stored.
- When you encounter something new, you handle it in working memory. If you want to move it to long term, it takes time and practice.
- To memorize things for long term memory, use _spaced repetition_.
- Spaced repetition is when you repeat on a schedule that decays in frequency over time.
- Repeating something many times in one evening is not nearly as effective as spaced repetition.
- If you don't give time for the connections to form, you won't have a good structure, as said previously.

### Lesson 4: The Importance of Sleep in Learning
- Being awake reates toxic molecules in your brain.
- When you sleep, your brain cells shrink, allowing brain fluid to wash away the toxins.
- Sleep is critical, therefore, not only for health, but for effective studying.
- PERSONAL ANECDOTE:
  - Never take a test without adequate sleep. I prefer 8 hours at least.
  - My number one strategy in school to handle an exam I didn't study for properly throughout elementary and high school was just to get a full night's sleep. That way, since my brain was functioning better than if I'd studied, I'd be able to just derive what I needed to know instead of actually having to learn the solutions to problems. I tended to test better than I did on assignments, therefore, because testing generally are less in-depth than assignments, and I never actually learned the material that well.
- Sleep is an insane nootropic for your ability to focus, make connections.
- Sleep erases less important and strengthens more important areas of memory, that you need (or want) to remember.
- Sleep also has a remarkable ability to help you figure out difficult problems and to understand what you are trying to learn.
  - It's as if the brain passively puts together a solution by searching different parts of the brain when the prefrontal cortex is not asserting itself.
- Of course, you must do focused mode work to prime the brain.
- If you study right before sleep you have an incresaed chance of dreaming about it.
- It consolidates your memories into easier to grasp chunks.
