* If you're like me, you stalled out after competitive programming, because there was a lack of structured problems with clear progression.
* This guide is designed to solve that problem: given someone who is generally competent at algorithmic programming inside a narrow API, this guide answers how they can genuinely develop useful skills that make them a formidable programmer in a variety of domains.
## 1. Get as good as is reasonable
The first step to transforming an aptitude for competitive programming into a real career is to first understand this simple fact: there is no more rapid technique of skill acquisition than short, structured problem tailored to your skill level. Therefore, it's essential that you extract as much utility out of competitive programming as possible. Don't just get good. Get really good. Ideally, get so good that they can't ignore you[^1]. Make writing correct, terse, performant, and readable programs the easiest part of the job.

Pick a language, ideally one with solid support and plenty of built-in data structures and algorithms. Some solid candidates are C++, Java, Python and Rust.[^2] After you've picked a language, learn its intricacies in detail -- the better you understand the language, the more programs you can write in it, which opens the door for further understanding. Learn its standard library. Learn common packages.

The goal is always to maximize the volume of possible programs you could theoretically write, while minimizing the surface area of syntax, libraries, and constructs you need to  learn, and then subsequently write those programs. A true master of music may spend their entire career learning only a handful of instruments, each of which can only play a handful of recognizable notes. And yet, there is tremendous complexity despite the small surface area. The degree to which repetition and recombination increases skill while minimizing time in wasted abstractions should not be underestimated.

Eventually, you will want to write some programs that do more than just answer algorithmic questions. Perhaps you will want to make a game, or an application, or perhaps a website. There is nothing wrong with these pursuits, but as soon as you start writing nontrivial software using libraries that are not built in, you're now in the domain of trying to achieve a result, rather than in the domain of continuously compounding knowledge and ability. For more on this, see the third bullet.
## 2. Pick a valuable niche
Eventually (usually due to necessity or interest), you will want to pick an area of programming to specialize in. Frontend, backend, distributed systems, machine learning, mobile, desktop, embedded, devops, infrastructure, systems programming. You may be tempted to try a lot of things, and that is alright, but understand the following fact:

* Once you become good enough to become economically valuable in a certain niche, anything that is not increasing yoru ability to longer increases your ability to learn. That is the curse of skill -- it increases the opportunity cost of all activities that are not that skill.
* Unless you're at a company that is intentionally underspecialized (such as being pre-product, where leanness and flexibility matters more), you will generally be hired for your area of expertise.

How do you go about picking a niche? Understand that not all niches are created equal. Some niches may be rapidly growing, and others shrinking; some may align with your interests, and others not at all. Some niches may also be fundamentally _more difficult_ than others, and thus demand a higher wage despite having fewer available positions. Try to invest the time to understand the landscape in detail -- talking to people who are further ahead in their careers than you are is a huge part of understanding what will be valuable, and what you will enjoy.

As a small bit of comfort, know that who you are working with often matters a lot more than what you are working on -- and normally it's easier to change jobs than it is to acquire a whole new skill set in tech. So, if you find a place with coworkers you like and the team is generally productive, and you're learning new things, hold on for dear life -- and ideally stay long enough and work hard enough to advance at least one formal level (i.e., get promoted at least once).
